## The Call Nobody Wants To Make

The appraisal lands. It's under the ARV you quoted at term sheet. Now you have to call your client and explain why the loan they were expecting isn't the loan they're getting — before they've heard it from anyone else.

This happens more than people admit. About 8% of home appraisals come in below the contract price, creating appraisal gaps where buyers may need to cover the difference or renegotiate, according to Redfin, published February 12, 2026. Investment-property valuations carry the same risk, and often more of it, because renovated comps are thinner and appraisers lean harder on historical sales that haven't caught up to the block yet.

The good news: a low appraisal resizes a deal. It rarely kills one. Here's what to say on the call, and the three structures that get the deal to closing anyway.

## Why Investor-Property Appraisals Miss ARV More Than Retail Ones

Appraisers work off comparable sales, and comps lag reality in fast-moving or thin markets. Markets are shifting unevenly in 2026: areas with newer homes are slowing while others strengthen, and a national appraisal model can't account for all that local nuance, HousingWire reported on April 13, 2026. For a fix & flip or ground-up deal, that means the appraiser may be pricing off pre-renovation comps in a neighborhood that's moved since the last closed sale.

The fix isn't to argue with the appraiser on the phone. It's to give them what they were missing before the report is final, and to know your fallback options before you dial your client.

## The Script: What To Tell Your Client In The First Five Minutes

Don't lead with the bad news. Lead with the plan.

1. **State the number and the source.** "The appraisal came back at [X], not [Y]. Here's the report."
2. **Name which cap governs.** Every term sheet sets two ceilings — a percentage of total project cost, and a percentage of appraised value. The lower one sets your loan amount. Point to which one just moved.
3. **Give them three ways forward**, not one. Resize, appeal, or restructure. Cover all three before they react to any one of them.
4. **Set a decision deadline.** A gap that sits unaddressed for a week costs more than one resolved in 48 hours. Ask for a decision within two business days.
5. **Point them to the math**, not your opinion. Run the new numbers through the [calculator](/calculator) so the client sees the resized loan, not just hears about it.

This is also the moment to pull up the file and confirm nothing else moved. Review what lenders check before term sheet against the appraisal — a value gap sometimes surfaces a second issue, like a scope of work that no longer matches the comps.

## Structure 1: Resize To The Lower Cap

This is the default, and it's not a failure — it's how the two-cap system is designed to work. Cost-based leverage and value-based leverage are separate limits, and whichever number is smaller governs the loan. If the appraisal drops the ARV, the value cap tightens and the loan resizes to it, even if the cost-based cap hasn't changed at all. That's the same logic that governs ground-up construction, where standard leverage runs 85% of full cost, 90% for builders with five or more completed ground-up projects, plus 5% of cost on top of either to finance the interest reserve — but after-completion value is always a second, separate cap, and the lower of the two wins. See [how loan-to-value and loan-to-cost interact](/blog/ltv-vs-ltc) for the full mechanics.

For your client, resizing usually means one of three things: more cash to close, a smaller renovation budget, or both. Walk them through fix and flip loan requirements so they know exactly what documentation supports a revised budget if the scope needs to shrink.

## Structure 2: Challenge The Number Before You Accept It

An appraisal isn't gospel. It's one licensed opinion, built on the comps and facts the appraiser had in hand. A value appeal is not a negotiation on price. It succeeds on overlooked comparable sales, factual errors in the report, or property features the appraiser didn't capture, not on the argument that the value is simply lower than the contract price, per Reggora, published August 18, 2026.

Build the strongest packet the first time. Pull three to five closed comps the appraiser may have missed, chosen the same way you would [calculate ARV](/blog/how-to-calculate-arv), document any renovation or permit history that wasn't visible at inspection, and flag factual errors directly. Timelines vary by lender, but in most cases, a Reconsideration of Value takes between 3 and 14 business days from submission to response, according to Appraisal Key, published January 7, 2026. Build that into your closing timeline conversation — you can still hit 5-10 business days if the appeal is filed fast and the packet is complete.

Set this expectation before the appraisal is ordered, not after it lands. When you walk a client through the term sheet, tell them which cap is likely to govern and what happens to proceeds if the value comes in light. A client who heard it from you first treats a low appraisal as a known risk, not a broken promise.

## Structure 3: Change The Deal, Not The Lender

Sometimes the appraisal is right and the deal needs to change instead. Three moves here:

- **Renegotiate purchase price** with the seller down to something the appraisal supports — this is often faster than an appeal.
- **Bring the gap in cash.** If the client has the reserves, closing on a resized loan with more money down keeps the timeline intact.
- **Plan the exit around the real number.** If this is a hold instead of a flip, a bridge loan now with a refinance hard money loan to DSCR later, once the renovation is done and a new appraisal reflects the finished product, can make more sense than fighting today's number.

Whichever path your client picks, the underlying pricing doesn't move because of a negotiation — it moves because underwriting, appraisal, title, and insurance all have to sign off. Terms are subject to underwriting, appraisal, title, and insurance.

## Quick Reference For The Call

| Situation | Best structure | What client needs |
|---|---|---|
| Comps were thin or stale | Value appeal | New comps, permit history, corrected facts |
| Budget just needs a trim | Resize to lower cap | Revised scope of work |
| Client has reserves | Cash-in | Proof of funds |
| Long-term hold anyway | Bridge now, refinance later | Patience and a completion appraisal |
