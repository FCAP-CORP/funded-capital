Most ground-up loans assume you'll sell. You build, list, close and pay off the loan. Build-to-rent changes the exit. You keep the house, and a long-term loan pays off the construction lender.

That means two loans sized on two different numbers. The construction loan is sized on cost. The DSCR takeout is sized on rent and appraised value. If you only plan the first loan, the second one makes your decisions for you.

## BTR in 2026: fewer starts, slower rent growth

The market got more selective this year. According to NAHB's Eye on Housing analysis, published August 2026, about 15,000 single-family built-for-rent homes were started in the second quarter of 2026, down from 18,000 in the same quarter of 2025. The same NAHB analysis counted 63,000 BTR starts over the trailing four quarters, a 16% drop from the 75,000 in the four quarters before that. NAHB blamed higher financing costs, more multifamily supply and policy worries about institutional capital.

Part of that policy worry has eased. NAHB reported in the same August 2026 piece that the housing law as enacted does not ban institutional capital from financing BTR.

Rents are the other half of the equation. Cotality's Single-Family Rent Index, released September 17, 2026, shows single-family rents up 1.8% year over year in July 2026, compared with 2.3% a year earlier. That same Cotality release shows high-priced rentals up 2.6% and low-priced rentals up only 0.6%.

What that means for a builder: don't count on rent growth. Underwrite today's rent. If the deal only works when rents climb during construction, you're building on hope.

## Phase one: the construction loan

This is our ground-up leverage structure as of September 23, 2026. It has three parts.

- **Standard: 85% of full cost.** Full cost means purchase price plus sunk costs plus the remaining construction budget.
- **20 or more completed ground-up builds: 90% of full cost.** Here's [how underwriters count completed deals](/blog/how-underwriters-count-completed-deals).
- **Interest reserve: a further 5% of cost on top of either number.** That's how an experienced builder reaches 95% all-in with the reserve financed. That 5% only pays interest. It never pays for construction.

After-repair LTV is a second cap. The loan is whichever of the two numbers is lower. If you're fuzzy on the difference, read [LTV vs. LTC](/blog/ltv-vs-ltc) first.

Here's the cost stack on a single build:

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Lot purchase | $100,000 |
| Sunk costs (plans, permits, site work) | $20,000 |
| Remaining construction budget | $280,000 |
| **Full cost** | **$400,000** |
| Standard loan at 85% | $340,000 |
| Loan at 90% (20+ completed builds) | $360,000 |
| Plus 5% interest reserve | $20,000 |
| **Experienced builder, 95% all-in** | **$380,000** |

*Illustrative. Not a quote. If the after-repair LTV cap produces a lower number, the lower number is the loan.*

Now the part that's different for BTR. A flipper stops paying interest at the sale. You stop paying at the DSCR closing. That includes lease-up time after the certificate of occupancy. Size the reserve so it covers both the build and the lease-up. Our [interest reserve guide](/blog/interest-reserve-ground-up-construction-broker-guide) walks through the math.

## Phase two: the DSCR takeout

DSCR is monthly rent divided by the monthly payment. That payment is PITIA: principal, interest, taxes, insurance and any association dues.

Say the finished house rents for $3,000 a month and PITIA is $2,500. DSCR is 1.20. *Illustrative.* For the full method, see [how to calculate DSCR](/blog/how-to-calculate-dscr).

New construction changes two lines in that payment.

**Taxes.** A finished house usually gets assessed higher than the lot it sat on. Today's lot tax bill is not your rental tax bill. Run the numbers with an estimate based on the finished value.

**Insurance.** The builder's risk policy ends. A landlord policy replaces it at a different premium. See [builder's risk vs. vacant dwelling](/blog/builders-risk-vs-vacant-dwelling) for the handoff.

Both lines feed PITIA. If either one comes in higher than you assumed, your DSCR drops.

**Rent source.** Ask your DSCR lender up front which rent it will use: a signed lease, the appraiser's market rent estimate, or the lower of the two. That answer can change your loan amount.

**Seasoning.** This is the line most BTR plans miss. A CrowdFunded Wealth research note published May 2026 says cash-out DSCR refinances usually need 6 months of seasoning, while rate-and-term refinances can need none. A DSCR seasoning guide by broker Mo Abdel, last reviewed June 5, 2026, says cash-out rules are usually stricter than rate-and-term and vary by lender.

For a build-to-rent deal, that means one decision. If the takeout only pays off the construction loan and closing costs, it usually counts as rate-and-term. If you also want to pull out the equity you created, you're probably in cash-out territory, and you may have to wait. Decide which one you're doing before you start the build. Our posts on [refinancing hard money into DSCR](/blog/refinance-hard-money-loan-to-dscr) and the [DSCR cash-out refinance](/blog/dscr-cash-out-refinance) go deeper.

**The payoff gap.** Back to the example. A $380,000 construction loan needs a takeout of at least $380,000 plus closing costs. If the DSCR loan comes in smaller, you cover the difference in cash. The max LTV and the rent coverage on the takeout decide that. Check both before you pour the slab.

## Underwrite the takeout before you build

1. **Price the lot from the rent.** Start with what the finished house rents for and work backward. See [residual land value](/blog/residual-land-value-price-the-lot-first).
2. **Pull rent comps on new product.** Use new builds near your lot, not 1970s houses.
3. **Build PITIA with taxes on the finished house and a landlord insurance quote.**
4. **Run DSCR on a current quote.** Our DSCR program starts from 6.0%. Get a real quote. Don't assume one.
5. **Compare the construction payoff to the takeout's max loan.** If the takeout can't cover the payoff, fix it now. Cut cost, add equity or change the product.
6. **Size the reserve for build time plus lease-up.**
7. **Check the prepayment terms on the takeout.** You'll live with them for years. See [DSCR prepayment penalties](/blog/dscr-loan-prepayment-penalty).

## Where BTR deals break

- **Projected rent growth.** With July rents up 1.8% nationally per Cotality, growth won't rescue a thin deal.
- **Tax shock.** The reassessment on the finished house lands in the first year of PITIA.
- **Lease-up outlasts the reserve.** Then the interest comes out of your pocket.
- **Cash-out on day one.** Seasoning can push it back by months.
- **The appraisal comes in light.** The after-repair LTV cap then sets the construction loan below the cost cap, and the takeout shrinks with it.
