Two policies. Both cover a house nobody lives in. One of them pays your claim and one of them doesn't, and most investors find out which is which in month four.

This is the most confused distinction in investor insurance, and it stops closings cold. Here's the short version: **vacant dwelling covers an empty house. Builder's risk covers a house you are rebuilding.** If a contractor is on site, you need builder's risk.

The longer version is where the money is.

## Vacant Dwelling: What It Covers, and Where It Stops

A vacant dwelling policy is built for a building that is sitting empty and static. No tenants, no work, no materials stacked in the living room. It covers the standard named perils — fire, wind, lightning, vandalism, sometimes theft — against the value of the structure as it exists on the day you bind.

That last part is the trap. A vacant policy is typically written to the as-is value, which on a flip is roughly your purchase price. It's the right product for the three weeks between closing and permit issuance. It is the wrong product the moment demo starts.

Two more things it generally won't do:

- **Cover materials.** The $18K of cabinets sitting in the garage is not part of the structure. A vacant policy doesn't see it.
- **Cover damage arising from construction.** Most vacant forms exclude loss during renovation outright, or suspend coverage once work exceeds a defined scope.

And a landlord policy is worse, not better. A DP-3 assumes an occupied building. Nearly all of them carry a vacancy clause that cuts coverage after 30 or 60 days empty. Investors who close on a rental, start a gut renovation, and leave the seller's policy structure in place are frequently uninsured without knowing it.

## Builder's Risk: Coverage While the Work Is Happening

Builder's risk is written for a property under active construction. It covers the structure, the work in progress, and — with the right endorsements — materials on site, materials in transit, and materials in temporary storage.

Limits are set to **completed value**: acquisition cost plus the full rehab budget. That's the whole point of the product. You are insuring what the building will be worth when the work is done, because that is what the carrier would have to rebuild.

Terms run in 6-, 9-, or 12-month blocks, usually with an extension option. Price the extension at bind time rather than at renewal — a project already behind schedule is a worse negotiating position than a project on day one.

Common endorsements worth asking about:

- **Soft costs.** Permit fees, architect fees, and loan interest you keep paying while the rebuild happens.
- **Delay in completion.** Covers carrying costs attributable to a covered loss.
- **Theft of materials.** Not always included by default, and copper still walks off job sites.

## Side by Side

| | Vacant Dwelling | Builder's Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Property state** | Empty, no active work | Active renovation or ground-up |
| **Limit set to** | As-is value (roughly purchase price) | Completed value (purchase + rehab) |
| **Materials on site** | Generally not covered | Covered, often with an endorsement |
| **Construction damage** | Usually excluded | Core coverage |
| **Typical term** | 3, 6, or 12 months | 6, 9, or 12 months, extendable |
| **Lender accepts on a rehab loan** | Rarely on its own | Yes, with mortgagee clause |

## When a Lender Requires One, the Other, or Both

On a loan with no rehab component — a straight bridge on a stabilized property — a vacant dwelling policy is often enough.

The moment there's a rehab holdback or a draw schedule, that changes. A lender funding construction dollars needs coverage sized to the construction dollars. At Funded Capital, any [fix and flip loan](/fix-and-flip-loans) with a rehab budget requires builder's risk written to completed value, and we'll want it before we fund, not after. Same on [ground-up construction](/new-construction-loans).

Both policies show up on the same deal more often than investors expect. You close on a Tuesday, permits land four weeks later, and that gap is vacant-dwelling territory. Some carriers write a renovation policy with a vacancy endorsement that covers both phases under one binder — cleaner, and worth asking for by name.

## The Three Lines Your Closer Actually Reads

An insurance binder is a closing condition, and it gets rejected for the same three things every time:

1. **Mortgagee clause.** The lender's exact entity name and mailing address, worded as the mortgagee. Not "additional insured." Get the wording from your loan closer and send it to your agent verbatim.
2. **Loss payee.** Names who gets paid on a claim. On a rehab loan, claim proceeds typically fund the repair through the same draw structure as the loan.
3. **Coverage amount.** Must meet or exceed the loan amount, and on a rehab deal it should meet completed value. A binder at purchase price on a loan that includes rehab dollars gets kicked back the day it arrives.

Get those three right and the binder clears in one pass. Get one wrong and you've spent two days you budgeted for underwriting on a document correction. ## Ordinance and Law on Older Stock

Buying 1960s and 1970s product? Add ordinance and law coverage.

Without it, a carrier pays to rebuild what existed. With it, the carrier pays to rebuild to current code — which on older Florida stock can mean a new roof deck, impact-rated openings, an upgraded electrical service, and revised egress. That gap has ended more projects than the original fire did. It's cheap as endorsements go, and it is the single most overlooked line on an investor binder.

> **Pro tip:** Bind builder's risk for the full ARV, not the purchase price. A total loss in month four is a claim against a finished-value asset. A policy written to the acquisition number leaves you short by exactly the amount you spent on the rehab.

## What the Gap Costs: An Illustrative Example

**Illustrative.** A $190K purchase with $75K of rehab, ARV $310K.

A vacant dwelling policy written at $190K. Fire in week ten, after $75K of work is complete and paid.

| | Written to $190K (vacant) | Written to completed value (builder's risk) |
|---|---|---|
| Covered structure value | $190,000 | $265,000 |
| Rehab dollars already spent | $75,000 | $75,000 |
| Recovery on a total loss | $190,000 | $265,000 |
| Investor's uncovered exposure | **$75,000** | **$0** |

Same fire. Same investor. The $75K difference was decided at bind, months earlier, by a single number on a declarations page. And because most rehab dollars are lender-funded through a draw schedule, that shortfall lands on a loan balance that doesn't shrink because the house burned. If you haven't mapped how draws release, read [how a rehab draw schedule works](/blog/rehab-draw-schedule) — the draw structure and the insurance limit should track each other.

## Insure the Finished House, Not the One You Bought

The rule is one sentence: if work is happening, the policy limit tracks completed value.

Everything else — the endorsements, the vacancy gap, the mortgagee wording — is detail around that one decision. Get the limit right and the rest is paperwork.
