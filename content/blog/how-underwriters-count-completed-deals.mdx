Ask five investors how many deals they've "completed" and you'll get five different answers. One counts a property still listed for sale. Another counts a refinance that hasn't recorded yet. A third counts a project a partner ran while they wrote the check. An underwriter counts none of that until it's proven — and the difference between "I've done this before" and a file that proves it is often the difference between standard leverage and the top tier.

This matters most on ground-up construction, where the leverage structure is built directly around a builder's completed-project count. It matters on fix & flip too, where pricing tightens as your documented history grows. Here's what actually counts, what doesn't, and how to build a file that reads the way an underwriter reads it — before you apply, not after a term sheet comes back lower than you expected.

## Why "Completed" Is a Defined Term, Not a Feeling

Industry underwriting guides increasingly separate borrowers by documented project count rather than self-reported experience. Experienced borrowers with 6 or more completed projects typically access 85-90% LTC and the best rates, and lenders build in experience-based rewards where the more projects you complete, the better your leverage and terms become. That reward only applies to projects the underwriter can verify — not projects you describe on a call.

Other lenders draw the line the same way. Lenders prefer borrowers who can document prior flips, such as completing at least one flip in the last 36 months, according to a fix-and-flip pricing analysis published January 2026. The word "document" is doing the work in that sentence. A deal you can't document doesn't move your pricing, no matter how real it was.

## The Completed-Deal Checklist

An underwriter is looking for a paper trail that proves three things: you owned the deal, you finished it, and you exited it the way you planned. That means:

- **A settlement or closing statement** showing the sale, or a refinance closing package moving the property to permanent or DSCR financing.
- **A final draw request and close-out** from the construction lender, or a certificate of occupancy for ground-up work.
- **Your name or your entity's name** on title, not a partner's or a general contractor's.
- **A timeline** — purchase date, completion date, and exit date — that shows the deal actually finished inside a reasonable window, not one still open two years later.

A project still under construction, still listed, or still waiting on a refinance to record doesn't clear the bar yet. It's real work, but it isn't proof yet. ## What Doesn't Count — And Why It's Not Personal

Underwriters exclude certain deals not because they doubt your work, but because the record can't support the claim:

- **Deals where you managed construction but didn't carry the capital risk.** Being the boots-on-the-ground GC on someone else's fund is valuable experience, but it isn't the same as underwriting risk for your own deal.
- **Deals that are still open.** An active rehab or a spec home mid-build shows intent, not a finished outcome.
- **Deals with a messy ending.** A completed deal that ended in foreclosure, a short sale, or a lender dispute is scored on the outcome, not just the closing date. This lines up with how brokers are told to vet lenders in general — a lender's track record during market stress tells you more than their marketing materials, and institutional-grade lenders maintained capital strategies during past downturns while many boutique operators froze their pipelines — the same scrutiny gets applied in reverse to a borrower's history.
- **Verbal or informal partnerships.** Without an operating agreement or a documented ownership percentage, a shared deal is hard to credit to any one person.

This isn't about penalizing a rough deal. It's about pricing risk on what's provable. A biz2credit review of common underwriting misses put it plainly: investors rarely get declined for deals that were done badly — it's the ones who came unprepared or unorganized that get declined. The lesson applies to your track record file the same way it applies to a single deal package: organize it, or it doesn't count.

## Why the Count Changes Real Money on Ground-Up

Ground-up construction is where completed-project count has the clearest dollar impact. Standard leverage runs 85% of full cost — purchase price, sunk costs, and the remaining construction budget combined. Builders with five or more completed ground-up projects move to 90% of full cost. On top of either number, 5% of cost can finance the interest reserve, which is how an experienced builder reaches 95% all-in — the reserve band pays for carry, not construction dollars. After-repair loan-to-value is a second, separate cap, and the lower of the two numbers governs regardless of tier.

That's a meaningful swing in cash-to-close on a mid-size build, and it's the reason underwriters push on documentation for every one of those five projects, not just the most recent one. ## Fix & Flip Follows the Same Logic, Just a Smaller Swing

On fix & flip, a documented track record doesn't unlock a fixed leverage jump the way ground-up does, but it does move pricing. ## Building the File Before You Apply

The fastest way to get full credit for your history is to build the file before an underwriter asks for it:

1. **Pull settlement statements** for every sale and refinance closing on prior projects.
2. **Gather final draw sheets and certificates of occupancy** for anything you built.
3. **Confirm your name or entity is on title** for each deal you want credited.
4. **Note the timeline** — purchase to exit — for each one.
5. **Separate clean exits from anything that didn't go to plan**, and be ready to explain the latter directly rather than leaving it out.

A file built this way reads the same to any underwriter, at Funded Capital or anywhere else. It's also the fastest way to know your real number before you apply, rather than finding out after a term sheet lands lower than expected. ## The Caps That Apply No Matter How Deep Your File Is

However strong the track record, two things don't move: credit and the second leverage cap. Most Funded Capital programs look for 660+ credit, with 680+ unlocking the best pricing and 640-659 reviewed case by case — a deep project history doesn't waive that review. And on ground-up, after-repair LTV remains a second cap alongside cost-based leverage; the lower of the two always governs, no matter how many completed projects sit in your file. Terms are subject to underwriting, appraisal, title, and insurance.
