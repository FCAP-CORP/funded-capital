The numbers work. The purchase price passes the [70% rule](/blog/70-percent-rule-house-flipping). The rehab budget looks sensible. The comps are tight. The lender still says no.

This happens more often than investors expect. Underwriters do check the math. Then they check whether the math survives a foreclosure, a fire, a stalled draw or a lien claim. A deal can pass the spreadsheet and still fail the file.

Four problems cause most of these declines: lien position, vacancy, contractor gaps and the borrowing entity. Here is how each one looks from the underwriter's side, and what you can fix before you apply.

## The math is only the first test

An asset-based lender asks one question first: if this goes wrong, does the property repay the loan? Your credit still matters. At Funded Capital, most programs look for 660+, the best tiers start at 680+, and 640-659 is reviewed case by case. A 760 score won't fix a lien problem, though. The collateral has to stand up by itself.

To see what gets reviewed before terms are issued, read [what lenders check before a term sheet](/blog/what-lenders-check-before-term-sheet).

## 1. Lien position: first has to mean first

Private lenders lend in first position. Every other document in the file gets checked against that. Here are the things that break it.

**Liens that jump the line.** "First recorded, first paid" has exceptions. Property taxes are the big one. Georgia's tax code, O.C.G.A. § 48-5-28 (2020 edition, published on Justia), says taxes are paid before "any other debt, lien, or claim of any kind." Most states treat unpaid property tax the same way. So delinquent taxes on a purchase have to be paid off at closing.

**Mechanics liens that relate back.** This one catches flippers. The Law Offices of C. Melody Davalos explain, in an article published December 1, 2025, that a construction lender who records before visible work begins typically holds priority. Contractors who started before the lender recorded may claim to come first. Handle's guide to California lien law, updated June 2026, puts it plainly: if work began before the lender recorded its deed of trust, that lender can end up behind lien claimants who record much later.

The practical rule: no demo, no deliveries and no "head start" before your loan records. Starting early is one of the fastest ways to turn a clean first lien into a question for title.

**Seconds you didn't mention.** A seller carryback or a private second has to be disclosed up front. Many lenders won't allow one. Where it is allowed, its payment counts in your carrying costs. An undisclosed second that turns up at title is a credibility problem, and those rarely recover.

**Municipal and code liens.** Open permits, code fines and utility liens don't always show up where investors look. Order a municipal lien search with your title work, not after it.

For more on title surprises late in a deal, see [what stalls a deal after the term sheet](/blog/what-stalls-a-deal-after-the-term-sheet).

## 2. Vacancy: an empty unit costs more than lost rent

An empty rental is not an automatic no. Many DSCR lenders use the appraiser's market-rent estimate when a unit is vacant. The real issue is how much that estimate is worth in your market.

According to the U.S. Census Bureau's quarterly vacancy release, published July 28, 2026, the national rental vacancy rate was 7.3% in Q2 2026. The same release puts the South at 9.5%, the Midwest at 6.9%, the Northeast at 5.9% and the West at 5.3%. Principal cities ran 8.0%.

Here is what that means for a file. Say your coverage ratio only clears when you use the top rent comp, and the market is softening. Expect pushback. Run your ratio at a rent the appraiser can defend. Our guide on [how to calculate DSCR](/blog/how-to-calculate-dscr) walks through the math.

**Insurance is the hidden vacancy risk.** An article in Rough Notes, published June 2000, describes the ISO commercial property form. It restricts coverage for perils like vandalism, theft and glass breakage once a building has been vacant for 60 consecutive days. Many landlord policies carry similar vacancy language. A flip or rental between tenants can be underinsured exactly when the lender needs coverage most. Bind the right policy before closing. See [builder's risk vs. vacant dwelling](/blog/builders-risk-vs-vacant-dwelling) for how the two differ.

## 3. Contractor gaps: the budget is a promise someone has to keep

An underwriter treats your contractor as part of the collateral. The ARV assumes the work gets done, on budget, to the finish level the appraiser priced in. If the contractor file can't support that, the ARV is a guess.

These are the gaps that sink files:

- **Lump-sum budgets.** "Kitchen: $40,000" tells nobody what's being bought. Line items do.
- **No license, or a license in someone else's name.** Check it for the trade and the state.
- **No insurance certificate.** Missing general liability coverage is a red flag on any scope.
- **Scope that doesn't match the ARV.** The comps show quartz and new systems. The budget pays for paint and carpet.
- **No permits or approved plans on ground-up work.** Without them there is no realistic start date.

Rehab money usually goes out in draws after the work is inspected. A weak contractor means stalled draws, and stalled draws mean holding costs. Our [contractor vetting checklist](/blog/contractor-vetting-documents-before-first-draw) covers the documents to collect before the first draw.

### Ground-up: the leverage assumes a believable budget

On ground-up loans, the budget drives the loan amount. At Funded Capital, standard leverage is 85% of full cost, meaning purchase price plus sunk costs plus the remaining construction budget. Builders with 20+ completed ground-up builds get 90% of full cost. On top of either, a further 5% of cost can finance the interest reserve. That band never pays for construction. So an experienced builder reaches 95% all-in with the reserve financed.

After-repair loan-to-value is a second, separate cap, and the lower of the two governs. Every one of those percentages assumes a budget the underwriter believes. If the cost line isn't supported, the number it multiplies isn't either. For how experience gets verified, read [how underwriters count completed deals](/blog/how-underwriters-count-completed-deals).

## 4. Entity age: a new LLC is fine, an undocumented one is not

Most investors borrow through an LLC, and many form a new one for each deal. Age alone is rarely the problem. The problem is what a new entity can't show yet:

- **Who owns it.** There's no operating agreement, or the members don't match the guarantors.
- **Who can sign.** Nothing authorizes the person signing the loan documents.
- **Good standing.** The LLC isn't registered in the state where the property sits.
- **Recent title moves.** The property went into the entity weeks before a cash-out request, which raises seasoning questions.

Your track record belongs to you, not the LLC. Deeds and settlement statements prove it. Our [LLC real estate loan guide](/blog/llc-real-estate-loan) covers the setup.

The rules have shifted, but the question hasn't. Ballard Spahr reported in August 2026 that FinCEN's final rule, issued August 11, 2026, permanently exempts U.S.-formed entities from beneficial ownership reporting. Sidley noted the same month that the 2016 Customer Due Diligence Rule still requires covered financial institutions to identify and verify the beneficial owners of entity customers. Filing with the government changed. A lender still needs to know who it is lending to.

## The deal on paper vs. the deal in the file

| Looks fine on paper | What the file shows | Fix before you apply |
|---|---|---|
| Clean purchase price | Delinquent taxes, open code liens | Title and municipal lien search on day one |
| Rehab starts fast | Demo began before the loan recorded | No work on site until closing |
| Strong market rent | Soft submarket, vacant unit, landlord policy | Defensible rent comps and a vacant policy |
| Solid budget | Lump sums, no license, no insurance certificate | Line-item bid, license, certificate, contract |
| Experienced borrower | New LLC with no operating agreement | Articles, operating agreement, good standing, signing resolution |

## How to get to yes faster

1. **Run the numbers honestly.** Use the [calculator](/calculator) with rent and ARV the comps can support.
2. **Clear title early.** Get payoff letters and lien waivers from anyone who has worked on the property.
3. **Keep the site quiet until the loan records.**
4. **Build the contractor file before you need it.**
5. **Send entity documents with the application.** Don't wait for the underwriter to ask.

**Brokers:** screen these four items before you submit. A file that arrives clean gets read first.
