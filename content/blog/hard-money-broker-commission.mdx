Most lenders will not print their broker commission. You have to ask, then negotiate, then find out at closing whether you got what you thought.

Ours: **up to 3% per closed loan**, paid at the table, disclosed on the term sheet. This post covers how that number is actually built, what it pays by loan size and product, when it hits, and the two things that can shrink it.

## How Broker Compensation Works on a Business-Purpose Loan

A business-purpose loan is a loan to an investor on non-owner-occupied property. That is a different animal from a consumer mortgage, and the compensation rules are different too.

Consumer mortgage brokering runs on the loan originator compensation rule — capped comp, no dual compensation, tightly prescribed disclosure. None of that machinery governs an investor loan on a rental or a flip in most states. What replaces it is contract: what the lender agrees to pay, papered before the loan closes.

That freedom cuts both ways. Nobody is setting your fee for you, and nobody is protecting it either. The broker who papers compensation early gets paid what was agreed. The broker who leaves it verbal finds out at closing.

Licensing is separate from compensation and it is genuinely state-by-state. Several states require no mortgage license for business-purpose lending; others license it; a few regulate the fee itself. Confirm your state before you take a fee — with counsel or the regulator, not with a forum post. Nothing here is legal advice.

## Front-End Points vs. Yield Spread

There are two ways a broker gets paid on a private loan, and they land on different people.

**Front-end points** are a fee the borrower pays at closing. It appears on the settlement statement. Everybody sees it.

**Yield spread** is paid by the lender out of a higher rate. The borrower pays nothing at closing and pays more every month for the life of the loan.

We pay on the front end and we disclose it on the term sheet. Two reasons. First, a borrower who finds out later that their rate was marked up does not send you a second deal — and broker economics are built on second deals. Second, front-end compensation is a fixed, known number. Yield spread on a bridge loan that pays off in seven months is a number nobody can quote you honestly up front.

## When You Get Paid — and What Can Cut It

Paid at closing, out of the settlement statement, same table as every other closing cost. No invoice, no net-30, no waiting on a funding cycle. If the loan does not fund, there is no commission. Business-purpose broker comp is paid on closed loans, period.

That is why submission quality is a compensation issue, not an administrative one. A complete file returns a term sheet in 2 hours. An incomplete one burns two days in back-and-forth, and in a market where inventory moves in under a month, two days is how a signed contract becomes a backup offer.

Two things move the number after the term sheet:

**The loan amount changes.** Commission is a percentage of what actually funds. An appraisal that lands under the sizing assumption, or a rehab budget an underwriter trims, drops the funded amount and drops your fee with it. A $600K file that closes at $520K pays on $520K. Watch the appraisal, not the contract.

**Dual compensation.** If you are also taking a fee from the borrower directly, say so before the term sheet goes out. Undisclosed double-dipping is where a broker relationship ends, and it is the one thing that will get a file pulled at the table.

Early payoff provisions exist at some lenders. Ask. It is a one-sentence question and the answer belongs in your file, not in your memory.

> **Pro tip:** Negotiate your commission before the term sheet goes out, not at the closing table. Compensation disclosed up front and papered in the term sheet is the version that survives a rushed closing. Every broker who has lost a fee lost it in the last 48 hours of a deal.

## Keep the Documentation Clean

Three documents and you are covered on almost every file:

1. **A broker agreement with the lender**, signed before you submit. It sets the ceiling and the mechanics once, so you are not renegotiating per deal.
2. **Commission stated on the term sheet** — the amount or the percentage, in writing, before the borrower signs.
3. **A settlement statement line item** that matches the term sheet. If those two numbers disagree, fix it before funding, not after.

Keep a copy of all three per closing. It takes four minutes and it is the difference between a commission you can prove and one you can only describe. The same discipline applies to [closing costs](/blog/hard-money-loan-closing-costs) generally — a broker who can explain every line on the statement is a broker whose borrower comes back.
