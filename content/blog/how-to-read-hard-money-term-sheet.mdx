Most investors read a term sheet in six seconds: find the rate, find the points, sign. Then the deal gets expensive in lines they never read.

A hard money term sheet is short — usually one or two pages. Every line on it moves money. At [Funded Capital](/apply) we turn term sheets around in about 2 hours, and this post is the annotated walkthrough we wish every borrower had before signing anyone's paper, including ours. Here are the eight lines that actually move your returns, in the order they appear.

## Line 1–3: Loan Amount, Initial Advance, Holdback — Three Different Numbers

The headline loan amount is a total facility, not a wire. What funds at closing is the **initial advance** — typically your leverage against the purchase price. The rehab budget lives in the **holdback**, released in draws as work passes inspection.

Two term sheets can show the same $320K loan amount while one wires $245K at closing and the other wires $220K. That $25K gap is your working capital. Read all three lines together, and check how draws are handled — our guide to [rehab draw schedules](/blog/rehab-draw-schedule) covers the mechanics.

**The tip that saves the most money: find the holdback line before the rate line.** A term sheet quoting a lower rate with 100% of rehab held back can cost more in carry than a higher rate with a funded first draw. Compare total dollars out, not headline rate.

Illustrative: a $315K purchase with an $85K rehab. Term Sheet A — from 9.25%, 2 points, rehab 100% held back. Term Sheet B — from 9.99%, 1.5 points, $25K first draw funded at closing. Over a six-month hold, B's funded draw shaves roughly three weeks off the schedule and lands within a few hundred dollars of A on total cost — while keeping $25K of working capital in the deal. The "cheaper" sheet wasn't.

## Line 4–5: Rate and Points — the Two Everyone Reads

Fine — read them. But read them as a pair. Points are prepaid cost: 2 points on a six-month loan annualizes to roughly 4% of extra rate. A 9.25% + 2pt loan and a 9.99% + 1.5pt loan are much closer than they look.

Context helps here too. With the 30-year mortgage at 6.65% (Freddie Mac PMMS, Aug 20, 2026), business-purpose bridge money pricing from the high 8s and low 9s isn't a markup on the same product — it's a different product priced off a different curve. Bridge pricing follows the short end (SOFR at 3.65%), while 30-year [DSCR money](/dscr-loans) follows the long end (10-year Treasury at 4.69%, Federal Reserve H.15, Aug 20, 2026). That's why refinancing a flip into a DSCR loan at stabilization drops your rate — the deal moves from one curve to the other.

The full pricing breakdown is in our [hard money loan rates guide](/blog/hard-money-loan-rates); every fee at the closing table is in [closing costs](/blog/hard-money-loan-closing-costs).

## Line 6: The Exit Fee Nobody Reads

Some lenders charge a fee when the loan *pays off* — often 0.5% to 1% of the loan amount, sitting quietly below the origination line. It costs nothing at closing, which is exactly why it's easy to miss and exactly when you should look for it. On a $320K loan, a 1% exit fee is $3,200 of your profit, collected on your way out.

Funded Capital doesn't play hide-the-fee: what you see in the term sheet is what you get at closing. Wherever you borrow, price the exit line into your total cost before you sign.

## Line 7: Extension Terms and Default Pricing

Every bridge loan has a maturity date, and flips run long — that's not pessimism, it's math you should price. The extension line tells you what happens when they do: typically a fee (often 0.5–1% per extension) for another three to six months at the note rate.

The line below it — default pricing — tells you what happens if you *can't* extend: rates stepping up several points, triggered by passing maturity, missed payments, unapproved scope changes, or lapsed insurance. A term sheet with a clearly priced extension option is a safety rail. One where the only path past maturity is the default rate is a trap with a nice rate on it. We wrote up the whole endgame in [what happens when your loan comes due](/blog/hard-money-loan-extension-maturity-default).

## Line 8: Conditions Precedent — the Speed Line

The last section lists what the lender needs before funding: entity docs and EIN letter, valuation, title commitment, insurance binder, contractor bid, proof of liquidity. This list is where "close in 5 days" is won or lost.

The lender controls the term sheet turnaround — ours is about 2 hours. You control the conditions list. A borrower with a seasoned LLC, comps, a bid, and an insurance agent on speed dial closes in days; a borrower forming an LLC after signing waits on the state. Same lender, same deal, weeks apart — the full timeline is in [how long it takes to close](/blog/how-long-to-close-hard-money-loan).

## The Sixty-Second Term Sheet Check

Before you sign any term sheet — ours included — run this pass:

| Line | The Question | The Trap It Catches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial advance | What actually wires at closing? | Headline loan amount ≠ day-one cash |
| Holdback | First draw funded, or 100% held? | Low rate, starved rehab schedule |
| Rate + points | What's the total over MY hold? | Points that outweigh a rate discount |
| Exit fee | What do I pay on the way out? | Profit clawed back at payoff |
| Extension | Priced option, or default rate only? | The 7th-month cliff |
| Conditions precedent | What can I assemble today? | The 25-day "fast close" |

Sixty seconds, six lines, and you're comparing loans on total dollars out instead of a headline number.
