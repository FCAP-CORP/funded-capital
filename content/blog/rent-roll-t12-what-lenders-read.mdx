A rent roll tells you what tenants owe. A T-12 tells you what they paid. The underwriter's job is to measure the gap between the two and explain every dollar of it.

If you're buying or refinancing a small multifamily or a rental portfolio, these two documents set your loan size before the appraisal even lands. This post covers what an underwriter reads on each one, how the two get tied together, and the patterns that make a package unreliable. Think of it as the document-level companion to our [multifamily loans guide](/blog/multifamily-loans-guide).

## Two documents, two different jobs

A StatementsReady explainer published August 2026 puts the split plainly: the rent roll captures contracted rent as of a single date, while the T-12 records what the property actually collected and spent, month by month, over the trailing year.

The rent roll is your income story going forward. One row per unit: unit number, tenant, lease start, lease end, contract rent, deposit, balance owed.

The T-12 is your income story looking back. Income minus operating expenses gives net operating income. NOI drives both the debt service coverage ratio and the income-based value. If those terms are new, start with [how to calculate DSCR](/blog/how-to-calculate-dscr) and [how to calculate cap rate](/blog/how-to-calculate-cap-rate).

Neither document proves anything alone. A lender believes the numbers once the two agree.

## What a lender reads on the rent roll

| Field | What the underwriter checks |
|---|---|
| Date and signature | Is it current? Did the owner certify it? |
| Unit count | Matches the T-12, the appraisal and the building |
| Contract rent vs. market | Below-market upside, or above-market rents that may reset |
| Lease start dates | A cluster of brand-new leases |
| Lease end dates | Too many expirations in the same few months |
| Month-to-month tenants | Turnover risk |
| Concessions column | Free rent that lowers effective income |
| Balance owed | Delinquency |
| Deposits | Agree with the signed leases |

Start with the date. Freddie Mac's Multifamily Seller/Servicer Guide, Chapter 60, in the version updated August 25, 2026, requires a rent roll dated within 30 days before the appraisal's as-is effective date, certified by the borrower as accurate and correct. Private lenders don't run on agency rules. The agency standard is still a fair benchmark for what "current" means.

Then look at who lives there and how long they've been there. A Proprise rent roll guide published February 2026 argues that a building at 95% occupancy with 80% new tenants carries more risk than one at 85% with long-term tenants. The same guide treats month-to-month concentration above 20% as a red flag in underwriting.

Occupancy is a number. Tenure is evidence.

## What a lender reads on the T-12

Underwriters read the monthly columns, not the annual total. A clean annual NOI can hide a bad trend.

MSA Data Insights, in a guide published May 2026, describes a property whose rental income ran flat for nine months, then jumped in the final quarter while concessions doubled. That's a building bought with free rent to look full at sale time. The annual total never shows it. The monthly columns do.

On the expense side, look for what's missing or out of date:

- **Insurance and taxes.** The same MSA Data Insights guide notes that insurance has repriced and property taxes are catching up to 2021 and 2022 sale comps. A T-12 carrying last year's premium overstates NOI.
- **Replacement reserves.** A Matrix Commercial Capital glossary entry published June 2026 says small-owner T-12s often leave out replacement reserves, and that careful underwriting adds roughly $300 to $500 per unit per year.
- **Management fee.** A self-managing owner often books zero. A lender will still charge the building for management.
- **Vague lines.** An Archer analysis published April 2025 flags loosely labeled items such as "Miscellaneous Income" or "Fees" as triggers for deeper diligence.

## The reconciliation: where files live or die

CREquity's 2026 underwriting guide, published August 2026, calls unexplained gaps between rent roll and T-12 income the most common deal-killer at prescreen. Here's what that check looks like on a small building.

*Illustrative example: 12 units, 11 occupied at $1,500 a month.*

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Rent roll: 11 occupied units x $1,500 x 12 | $198,000 |
| T-12 collected rental income | $176,000 |
| Gap | $22,000 (11.1% of the annualized rent roll) |

An 11.1% gap with no explanation kills your credibility. Now explain it:

| Explanation | Amount |
|---|---|
| Two new leases signed with one month free ($1,500 x 2) | $3,000 |
| One tenant four months behind ($1,500 x 4) | $6,000 |
| One unit vacant five months before it leased ($1,500 x 5) | $7,500 |
| Five renewals raised rent $100 in the latest month; the T-12 carries the old rent for 11 months (5 x $100 x 11) | $5,500 |
| **Total explained** | **$22,000** |

Every dollar is accounted for. The lender can now judge each piece on its merits. The rent increases are real if the new leases back them up. The delinquent tenant is a real risk and may be underwritten at zero. The free rent tells the underwriter what it costs to fill a unit in this submarket.

Same building, same numbers. One file reads as messy. The other reads as an owner who knows the property.

## Seven signs a package is unreliable

1. **The documents disagree on unit count.** MSA Data Insights says the latest T-12 month and the latest rent roll should agree on unit count, occupancy and rental income.
2. **The gap has no explanation.** Every dollar between the rent roll and collections needs a reason.
3. **A wall of new leases.** Most leases started in the last few months usually means occupancy was bought for the sale.
4. **Concessions that don't appear on the rent roll.** Tilt Analytics, in a guide published July 2026, shows that a $1,950 asking rent with one month free on a 12-month lease collects an average of $1,787.50 a month. AcquiOS, writing in April 2026, recommends pulling the actual leases for any unit signed in the 90 days before the rent roll date.
5. **A year-end spike.** Income jumps late in the year with no matching rent increases on the roll.
6. **Round-number or missing expenses.** No management fee, no reserves, repairs that look like round estimates.
7. **Rents well above market.** Above-market contract rents tend to reset at renewal, and the lender will haircut them.

## Why underwriters read harder in 2026

The market gives them reason to. Apartment List's national rent report puts multifamily vacancy at 7.0% as of September 2026, down from a 7.3% peak in February 2026. Apartments.com reported in April 2026 that 41.2% of multifamily properties nationwide were offering rent concessions, up 9.9 percentage points from 2025.

When roughly four in ten properties offer concessions, a rent roll with no concessions column gets questions.

Vacancy floors apply even to full buildings. The StatementsReady explainer reports that Freddie Mac's guide does not allow an underwritten vacancy and collection-loss rate below 5%, and requires a higher rate when conditions warrant. A 100% occupied rent roll still gets a vacancy deduction.

## How to send a package that survives underwriting

1. **Date and sign the rent roll.** Keep it current. Include lease start, lease end, concessions and balances.
2. **Send the monthly T-12.** Not an annual summary. Add the prior year's statement if you have it.
3. **Write a one-page reconciliation.** Walk the annualized rent roll down to collected income, line by line, like the table above.
4. **Attach leases for recent move-ins** and any unit with a concession.
5. **Back the T-12 with bank statements** so deposits tie to reported collections.
6. **Update insurance and taxes** to current quotes and bills.

A clean package doesn't change what the building earns. It changes how fast a lender believes it.
