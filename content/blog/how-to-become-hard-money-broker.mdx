Most people who broker real estate loans never touch a hard money file. They should.

Business-purpose lending pays **up to 3% per closed loan**, most states don't require a mortgage license to place one, and the same investor client comes back four to six times a year. A consumer mortgage borrower refinances once a decade. An active flipper needs a lender every quarter.

This is how the role actually works — the licensing question, the pipeline, the packaging, and the money.

## What a Business-Purpose Loan Broker Actually Does

You find the deal, package the file, and place it with a lender who can close it. That's the job.

What you don't do is underwrite, fund, or service. The lender takes the credit risk. You're paid for origination — for bringing a fundable deal and for making it easy to fund.

The distinction that matters is who the borrower is and what the property does:

| | Consumer mortgage broker | Hard money / private money broker |
|---|---|---|
| **Borrower** | Individual, owner-occupant | Entity (LLC, corp), investor |
| **Property** | Primary residence, second home | Non-owner-occupied investment |
| **Rule set** | TRID, RESPA, NMLS licensing | Business-purpose; state-by-state licensing |
| **Underwriting driver** | Debt-to-income, W2 income, tax returns | Asset value, exit strategy, sponsor experience |
| **Typical timeline** | 30–45 days | 5–10 business days |
| **Repeat frequency** | Once every 5–10 years | 2–8 deals per year, per client |

That last row is the whole business case. Investor lending is a relationship business with a short repeat cycle, which means your pipeline compounds instead of resetting.

## Licensing: Where Business-Purpose Sits

Here is the short version, and then the caveat that matters more than the short version.

Federal consumer mortgage rules — TRID, RESPA, the SAFE Act's NMLS licensing requirement — apply to loans made to a natural person for personal, family, or household purposes. A loan made to an LLC, secured by a rental or a flip the borrower will never live in, is a business-purpose loan. Those federal consumer requirements generally don't reach it.

State law is where it gets specific. Some states license the brokering of any mortgage loan regardless of purpose. Others exempt business-purpose lending entirely. The states brokers ask us about most often are California, Arizona, Nevada, Minnesota, North Dakota, Oregon and Vermont — and requirements there have changed more than once in the last five years.

Three rules keep you clean:

1. **Confirm your own state before you take a fee.** Your state financial regulator or a licensing attorney, not a forum post. This costs an hour and protects the whole business.
2. **Never stretch the business-purpose label.** A loan on the borrower's primary residence is a consumer loan no matter what entity holds title or what the borrower says they'll do with the money. If it smells consumer, it is consumer.
3. **Paper the purpose.** A signed business-purpose certification in every file, every time.

One more practical point: licensing follows the property, not your desk. A broker in Florida placing a loan on an Ohio rental is working under Ohio's rules, not Florida's. If you intend to work across state lines — and most investor brokers eventually do — map the licensing position of your top five target states before you market into them, not after a file shows up.

## Building the Referral Pipeline

Brokers who struggle are usually waiting for borrowers to find them. Brokers who do volume go to the people who meet the borrower first.

**Real estate agents working distressed inventory.** The agent with three pocket listings that need $80K of work each has borrowers who can't get bank financing. That agent loses the commission if the buyer can't close. Be the reason the deal closes and you get the next one.

**Title and escrow officers.** They see investor purchases before anyone else and they know which buyers are cash-constrained. They're also the fastest way to learn who's actually active in your market versus who talks about it.

**General contractors.** A GC whose client runs out of money in week seven has a problem you can solve with a gap or bridge placement. That's a warm call, not a cold one.

**CPAs and property managers.** CPAs see the whole portfolio at tax time and know who's refinancing. Property managers watch owners decide between selling and pulling cash out — which is a [DSCR cash-out](/dscr-loans) conversation waiting to happen.

The mistake is treating these as one-time asks. Send the agent a copy of the term sheet when the deal funds. Send the title officer the closing. Referral sources send the second deal to whoever made the first one look good in front of their client.

## How to Package a File That Underwrites on the First Pass

This is the part that separates a broker who earns 3% from someone who forwards emails.

**Pro tip: lead with the exit, not the borrower.**

Underwriters read exit strategy first on business-purpose loans. Everything else — credit, experience, liquidity — is a question about whether the borrower can reach that exit. A submission that opens with a credible take-out moves faster than one that opens with a borrower bio.

A credible exit is one of three things:

- **Sale:** three comparable sales closed within 90 days and half a mile, supporting the after repair value. Not six stale comps from a wide radius.
- **Refinance:** a DSCR pre-qualification showing the property debt-services at the projected rent.
- **Existing contract:** a signed listing agreement or an executed sale contract.

Then the rest of the package:

| Document | Why the underwriter needs it |
|---|---|
| Executed purchase contract | Sets the basis and the closing deadline |
| Scope of work + contractor bid | Sizes the rehab holdback and the draw schedule |
| 3 ARV comps with adjustments | Tests the exit value |
| Entity docs: articles, operating agreement, EIN letter | Confirms the borrower and the business purpose |
| Schedule of real estate owned | Sets the experience tier |
| Liquidity verification | Proves the borrower can carry an overrun |

Send the ARV comps as a one-page grid with adjustments, not a raw MLS export. An underwriter who has to build the valuation argument for you is an underwriter who takes an extra day.

One note on credit, because brokers over-worry about it. Most programs want a **660+ mid-FICO**, the best pricing and leverage tiers open at **680+**, and **640–659 is case-by-case** against a strong equity position or track record. There is no hard floor at 680. A 650 borrower with real equity and a clean exit is a fundable file — see [hard money loan rates](/blog/hard-money-loan-rates) for how the score shows up in pricing rather than in approval.

## Getting Paid: Structure, Timing, Disclosure

**Structure.** Commission is points on the front, paid out of loan proceeds at closing, disclosed on the settlement statement. It runs 1% to 3% of the loan amount. Funded Capital pays up to 3% per closed loan.

The percentage generally moves inversely with size. A $250K fix and flip carries a higher point count than a $1.4M multifamily bridge, because the work per file is roughly the same and the dollars are not.

**Timing.** You're paid at closing. Not at term sheet, not at approval, not at rate lock. If the appraisal comes in short, title can't be cleared, or the borrower walks, there is no commission. That's the honest version, and it's why pre-screening the exit is worth more than chasing volume.

**Disclosure.** Negotiate your compensation before the term sheet goes out, not at the closing table. Compensation agreed up front and papered in the term sheet is the version that survives a rushed closing. Every broker dispute we've seen started as an undocumented handshake.

## Why the Timing Is Good

The private lending market is fragmented, and fragmentation is what makes a broker valuable.

Forecasa's count, published via the American Association of Private Lenders on October 15, 2025, put the field at **7,565 active private lenders — up 20% year over year**. Seven thousand lenders with different footprints, different credit boxes, and different closing speeds is not a market a borrower can shop on their own. They'll call three, get three different answers, and pick badly. That gap is the broker's product.

## Three Mistakes That Cost New Brokers Deals

**Shopping the file to eight lenders at once.** It feels like diligence. It reads as a distressed deal. Appraisal orders get duplicated, the borrower gets three different ARVs, and every lender assumes someone else already declined it. Pick two lenders whose credit box actually fits the deal and send it to both.

**Quoting a rate before the deal is sized.** The borrower hears the number you said, not the range you meant, and every term sheet after that feels like a bait-and-switch you caused. Quote the program range, say plainly that pricing follows the deal, and let the term sheet do the work.

**Disappearing after closing.** The commission is the smallest part of the transaction. The borrower who just closed in eight days is about to buy again in ninety, and the agent who referred them is watching how you handled it. Send the closing statement. Ask what the next deal looks like. That call is the highest-return fifteen minutes in the business.

## Your First 90 Days

A realistic ramp, not a guru timeline:

1. **Weeks 1–2.** Confirm your state licensing position in writing. Sign up with two or three lenders whose footprints and products differ — one for flips, one for rentals, one for ground-up.
2. **Weeks 3–6.** Build the referral list: ten agents working distressed inventory, three title officers, five contractors. Meet them in person where you can. Ask what kills their deals.
3. **Weeks 7–12.** Place your first file. Take one deal all the way through — submission, term sheet, appraisal, closing — before you chase the next five. The first complete file teaches more than twenty conversations.
