A draw inspection answers one question: does the work on site match the dollars you asked for?

If it does, the money moves. If it doesn't, you front the difference out of pocket while your contractor waits. Here is what the inspector checks, where draws come back short, and how to fix each problem before you hit submit.

## Who the Inspector Works For

Start here, because it changes how you prepare. According to DrawStack, published May 5, 2026, the inspector is hired by the lender and their job is to protect the lender's capital, "not to approve your draw."

The same DrawStack piece says the lender will typically fund at the inspector's percentage when it comes in lower than your claim, and the shortfall rolls into the next draw. That's the real cost of a failed draw. The money isn't lost. It's late, and you carry it until then.

The good news: inspectors are not hunting for tiny errors. Levelset, published April 18, 2023, notes that most draws are billed on a percentage-of-completion basis and that small discrepancies will probably be overlooked. Big gaps are what get flagged.

## Percent Complete: Where Most Draws Fail

Percent complete is physical. It is not what you've paid, and it is not how long the crew has been on site. Trinity Online, published June 16, 2026, puts it plainly: progress is "based entirely on verified physical completion."

That catches investors in three places:

- **Paid but not done.** You paid the framer 80% up front. The walls are 60% up. The inspector writes 60%.
- **Delivered but not installed.** Windows on pallets in the garage are not windows in the openings.
- **Lumped line items.** One big "interior finishes" line makes partial progress hard to measure, so the inspector reads it low.

Here is what that looks like on a single draw.

| Line item | Budget | You claimed | Inspector saw | Funded |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Framing | $60,000 | 80% ($48,000) | 60% | $36,000 |
| Rough plumbing | $18,000 | 100% ($18,000) | 100% | $18,000 |
| Windows | $22,000 | 50% ($11,000) | 0% (on pallets) | $0 |
| **Total** | | **$77,000** | | **$54,000** |

*Illustrative example, not a quote.*

You asked for $77,000 and received $54,000. That $23,000 gap is now your cash problem until the next draw.

On stored materials, Northwest Construction Control, published June 16, 2026, warns that relying on invoices alone exposes lenders to inflated or fabricated claims. So assume stored materials don't count until your lender confirms in writing that they do.

**The fix:**

1. Build the budget in small, measurable lines at underwriting. Our guide on [how to estimate rehab costs](/blog/how-to-estimate-rehab-costs) shows how to break it down.
2. Request a line when it's finished, not when it's half done.
3. Walk the site with your GC the day before you submit. Claim what you both see.
4. Match your draw milestones to your [rehab draw schedule](/blog/rehab-draw-schedule) so every request lines up with a visible stage.

## Photos That Help, Photos That Stall

Photos support a draw. They rarely carry it alone. Trinity Online, published June 16, 2026, cautions that photos can be "outdated, selective, or lacking context," which is why many lenders still send someone on site.

Quality matters too. In a July 10, 2024 post, Trinity Online says blurry or undersized photos can send the inspector back to the property for better evidence. A second trip means a second wait.

What a clean photo set looks like:

- **One wide shot, then one close-up, per budget line.** The wide shot proves which room. The close-up proves the work.
- **Label by budget line.** "Framing, 2nd floor" beats "IMG_4471."
- **Shoot rough-ins before they get covered.** Once drywall goes up, nobody can verify the plumbing or electrical behind it.
- **Fresh photos only.** Never resend last month's pictures.

Some private lenders lean heavily on photos. Cetan Funds, published June 10, 2026, says it can approve most draws on documentation and photos of completed work. Ask your lender at closing what it accepts so you can shoot the right set from day one.

## Lien Waivers: The Paper Side of the Draw

You can pass the site visit and still stall on paperwork. Lien waivers prove the people who did the work got paid, so none of them can put a lien on the property.

There are two kinds that matter on every draw. According to Built, published August 11, 2026, a conditional waiver takes effect only once payment is received and cleared, while an unconditional waiver takes effect immediately when signed.

Corpay, published June 11, 2026, explains that every progress payment needs its own conditional-then-unconditional pair, matched to the dollar amount. The same article warns that a skipped waiver mid-job leaves a gap the lender will catch on the next draw request.

State rules add another layer. Corpay lists eight states that require statutory waiver language, including Florida, Texas and California. DocJoist, published March 1, 2026, counts 12. Either way, a generic template in a statutory state is a risk. Siteline, published April 1, 2026, adds that Mississippi and Wyoming require waivers to be notarized.

Florida investors have one more step. Under the 2025 Florida Statutes, section 713.13, a lender must record the notice of commencement before disbursing construction funds to the contractor. FileAndGo, published March 12, 2026, notes that lenders typically want a certified copy of the recorded notice before the first draw.

The waiver mistakes that stall draws most often:

- A generic form used in a statutory-form state.
- A waiver amount equal to the gross billed instead of the current payment due, an error BuildWorkPro flagged in a September 2026 guide.
- Missing waivers from subs and suppliers, not just the GC.
- Through-dates that don't line up with the draw period.

## Timing Between Draws

Every draw costs you a fee and some days. Plan around both.

Turnaround varies widely by lender. Gelt Financial, published January 21, 2026, cites 3 to 5 days from request to funding with good preparation, and $100 to $300 per inspection. Cetan Funds, in its June 10, 2026 post, says it processes most draws in 24 to 48 hours and typically allows up to two draws per month.

Meanwhile you're floating the work. American Heritage Lending, published March 12, 2026, notes that borrowers usually pay for work up front before requesting a draw, so you need working capital between releases.

Too many small draws burn fees and time. Too few big ones strain your cash. Aim for draws at clear milestones: foundation, framing, rough-ins, drywall, finishes.

Two timing habits that help:

- **Submit the package first.** DrawStack recommends sending the draw package before the inspection is scheduled so the lender can dispatch the inspector right away.
- **Count back from maturity.** A slow final draw can push you past your loan term. Read our guide on [extensions, maturity and default](/blog/hard-money-loan-extension-maturity-default) before you get close, and track the carry in your [holding costs](/blog/holding-costs-house-flip).

## Ground-Up: Know What the Draws Can Buy

On ground-up loans, how your leverage is built shapes how your draws work. Here is how Funded Capital sizes it:

- **Standard: 85% of full cost.** Full cost means purchase price plus sunk costs plus the remaining construction budget.
- **Builders with 20 or more completed ground-up builds: 90% of full cost.**
- **Interest reserve: an extra 5% of cost on top of either number.** That takes an experienced builder to 95% all-in with the reserve financed.
- **After-repair loan-to-value is a second cap.** The lower of the two governs.

The key point for draws: the reserve band pays interest only. It never buys construction dollars. If a draw comes back short, the reserve doesn't cover the gap. Your cash does.

## The Pre-Submission Checklist

Run this before every draw:

1. Walk the site with your GC and agree on percent complete per line.
2. Claim installed work only unless your lender approved stored materials in writing.
3. Shoot a wide and a close-up photo for each line, labeled by budget line.
4. Photograph rough-ins before they get covered.
5. Collect conditional waivers from the GC, subs and suppliers, matched to the current payment.
6. Confirm the unconditional waivers from the last draw are in the file.
7. Use the statutory waiver form if your state requires one.
8. Give the inspector site access and a phone number that answers.

Do this every time and short draws get rare.
