You can re-bid a framer. You can swap out finishes. You cannot renegotiate the lot after closing.

Land is the one line in a ground-up budget that is locked the day you sign. Every other number has to fit under it. So your lot basis is the whole deal.

This post shows how to price land the way builders and lenders do: backward, from the finished house. For the full spec-build loan walkthrough, read our [spec home construction loan guide](/blog/spec-home-construction-loan). Here we stay on the dirt.

## Start at the Finished House

Residual land value (RLV) changes the question. You don't ask what this lot is worth next to other lots. You ask what the finished home sells for. Then you subtract every cost of getting there, your profit included. What's left is the most you can pay for the land.

FNRP, in a guide published November 27, 2024, describes the calculation as gross development value minus total project cost, with fees and developer profit counted in that cost.

For a single-family spec build, the formula looks like this:

**Residual land value = ARV - (hard costs + soft costs + contingency + financing and holding + selling costs + target profit)**

One rule matters more than the rest. Profit goes in as a cost, before you get to the land. A worked example from Domira, published August 2, 2026, warns that if profit is simply the leftover, you "will always be able to justify the asking price." That's the trap. The seller's number starts to look fine because you let your margin shrink to fit it.

## A Worked Example

The figures below are illustrative. Use your own comps and your own bids. If your ARV is soft, fix that first with our guide on [how to calculate ARV](/blog/how-to-calculate-arv).

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| ARV (finished sale price) | $650,000 |
| Selling costs (6% of ARV) | -$39,000 |
| Hard costs (2,000 sq ft at $175) | -$350,000 |
| Soft costs (plans, permits, fees, survey, insurance) | -$40,000 |
| Contingency (5% of hard costs) | -$17,500 |
| Financing and holding (12 months) | -$45,000 |
| Target profit (15% of ARV) | -$97,500 |
| **Residual land value** | **$61,000** |

Take out about $3,000 in closing costs on the land purchase, and your maximum offer is about $58,000.

Now say the seller wants $90,000. Pay it and nothing in the table changes except your profit. With the same $3,000 in closing costs, profit falls by $32,000, from $97,500 to $65,500. That's a 10.1% margin on a 12-month build.

For context, NAHB's 2024 Cost of Constructing a Home survey, published January 20, 2025, put average pre-tax builder profit at 11.0% of the sale price. Overpaying for the lot pushes this deal below the national average before the first footing is poured.

## Stress the Two Numbers You Don't Control

You don't set the sale price, and you don't set lumber prices. So run the residual again with each one moving against you. Profit stays at $97,500 in dollars.

| Scenario | Residual land value | % of base case |
|---|---|---|
| Base case | $61,000 | 100% |
| Hard costs up 6.7% (+$23,450) | $37,550 | 62% |
| ARV down 5% to $617,500 | $30,450 | 50% |

A 5% miss on the sale price cuts the lot's value in half. That's the key point of residual pricing. Land is what's left after everything else, so it takes the full hit from any small miss above it.

Neither stress case is far-fetched right now. NAHB's September 2026 builder survey found 38% of builders cut prices in September, and the average cut held at 6% for the sixth month in a row. On costs, NAHB reported on August 26, 2026 that the median builder saw a 6.7% annual rise in material costs for the same house. Small operators had it worse. HousingWire reported on August 26, 2026 that builders with five or fewer starts saw a median increase of 9.1%, versus 1.8% for builders with 100 or more.

So price the lot off your stressed case, not your base case. If the deal only works when everything goes right, the lot is overpriced.

## Why Leverage Hides an Overpaid Lot

Here is how our ground-up leverage works:

- **85% of full cost** as standard. Full cost means purchase price plus sunk costs plus the remaining construction budget.
- **90% of full cost** for builders with 20 or more completed ground-up builds. See [how underwriters count completed deals](/blog/how-underwriters-count-completed-deals).
- **Plus 5% of cost** on top of either number to finance the interest reserve. An experienced builder reaches 95% all-in with the reserve financed. That band pays for the interest reserve only. It never pays for construction.
- **After-repair loan-to-value is a second cap.** Whichever limit is lower governs.

Now run both lot prices at standard leverage. Illustrative figures, using the $407,500 build budget from the table (hard costs, soft costs and contingency):

| | Lot at $58,000 | Lot at $90,000 |
|---|---|---|
| Full cost | $465,500 | $497,500 |
| Loan at 85% | $395,675 | $422,875 |
| Your cash (before closing costs and carry) | $69,825 | $74,625 |
| Profit at sale | $97,500 | $65,500 |

Paying $32,000 more for the lot costs you only $4,800 more in cash at closing. That's why overpaying feels cheap on closing day. It isn't cheap. The full $32,000 comes out of your profit when you sell.

The ARV cap makes it worse. A higher lot price raises your cost basis without adding a dollar to the finished value. The more you overpay, the more likely the ARV cap becomes the limit that governs. For how the two caps interact, read [LTV vs. LTC](/blog/ltv-vs-ltc).

## What the Lot Market Looks Like Now

National numbers won't price your lot. They do show the squeeze.

- NAHB's 2024 cost survey, published January 20, 2025, found finished lot costs fell from 17.8% of the sale price in 2022 to 13.7% in 2024. In the same survey, construction costs rose to 64.4% of the sale price. Building is taking a bigger share of each sale dollar, which leaves less room for land.
- NAHB's Eye On Housing reported in July 2026 that the U.S. median lot value for homes started in 2025 was $59,000, compared with $60,000 a year earlier. The same analysis found record highs in the Pacific, Mountain, Middle Atlantic and East North Central divisions.
- NAHB's September 2026 Housing Market Index release noted that 42% of builders rated current lot availability as poor and 38% as fair.

Put those together. Lots are scarce, so sellers hold firm on price. Builders are cutting sale prices, so ARVs are under pressure. The residual gets squeezed from both ends. Your comps have to be local, recent and for new construction.

## Diligence That Changes the Residual

Most of what lowers a lot's residual value is visible before you sign. Check these during your contract period:

- **Zoning and setbacks.** How many square feet you can build drives ARV. A lot that fits 1,800 square feet instead of 2,000 is a different deal.
- **Utilities and tap fees.** No sewer or water at the street means site costs your budget may not include.
- **Impact and permit fees.** These go in soft costs. Get the actual schedule from the local jurisdiction.
- **Survey, flood zone and soils.** Fill, retaining walls or elevation requirements raise hard costs and insurance.
- **Access and title.** Easements and encroachments can stall a closing.

Regulation is a real cost. Builder and Developer reported on June 11, 2026 that NAHB's 2026 study put regulatory costs at $131,734 of a new home's price, with $46,795 of that coming from a higher finished lot price.
